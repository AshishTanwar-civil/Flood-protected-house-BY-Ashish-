Sec. 01 — Overview

Grounded on a dry day. Floating on a flood day.

Most flood defences fight the water — walls, pumps, sandbags. An amphibious foundation works with it instead: the house keeps its normal connection to the ground, and only lifts when a flood actually shows up, settling back to the exact same spot once it drops.

  • An architecture professor began developing amphibious foundations for flood-prone Louisiana homes after Hurricane Katrina in 2006 (the Buoyant Foundation Project), and the method has since been adapted for communities in Nicaragua, Jamaica, and rice-farming villages in Vietnam.
  • A whole neighbourhood of amphibious homes in Maasbommel, Netherlands sits on concrete foundations with guide posts and can rise as much as 5.5 metres when the Meuse river floods. When an actual flood hit in January 2011, every home rose as designed and settled safely back down.
  • A 2022 paper in an Indian engineering research journal already proposed amphibious house designs as a flood-mitigation strategy for India — a genuine published starting point for your literature review.

Why it works for a first-year team

Physics you already know

Archimedes' principle and basic foundation thinking — no structural analysis software required.

Cheap, safe materials

Foam board, thermocol, dowels and a plastic tub. Nothing hazardous, nothing that needs a workshop.

Genuinely demonstrable

Lower it into a tub, raise the water, and it visibly rises on camera — a two-minute demo no other group will have.

How it fits together

Underneath the house sits a sealed, buoyant base. As floodwater rises, that base displaces water and lifts the whole house upward. Two or more guide posts stop it drifting sideways, so it comes back down to exactly the same spot once the water drops.

  • 1House structure — lightweight walls and roof, built to sit flat on the base.
  • 2Flotation base — sealed and watertight, sized to displace enough water to carry the house's weight.
  • 3Guide posts — anchored into the ground, keep the rise perfectly vertical.
  • 4Anchor plate — fixes the posts to "ground," standing in for a real pile foundation.

The model: a constant-cross-section flotation base of footprint area A and thickness H, water density ρ = 1 g/cm³. Required submerged depth to float is d = weight ÷ A (from F = ρgV). Below that water level the house rests on the ground; once water passes it, the house rises 1:1 with the water so the base stays submerged exactly d deep — the same equilibrium math your Phase 00 calculation uses, just running live. Sec. 03 — Flood Impact

Why this is worth taking seriously

Flooding isn't an abstract design constraint for a class project — it's one of India's most consistent disasters, and one of the deadliest hazard types on Earth. These figures are from public disaster-tracking sources; use them to frame your problem statement.

This model won't fix flooding at national or global scale. But the same principle it demonstrates — letting a structure rise with the water instead of being destroyed by it — is a real, low-cost strategy already protecting homes in flood-prone places. The numbers below are the reason that strategy matters.

Sec. 06 — Testing & Data

Prove that it actually floats — and floats straight

This is the part evaluators will remember. Record real numbers from the physical model, not just a video of it working once.

Archimedes' Principle · Quick Reference
F = ρwater × g × Vdisplaced

A floating object displaces water equal to its own weight. For quick model-scale estimates: since ρwater ≈ 1 g/cm³, the volume (cm³) your flotation base must displace is roughly equal to the total weight (g) it needs to support. Build in a 50–100% margin for stability and test loads — or check your numbers first in the Simulation section above.
